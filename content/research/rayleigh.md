Most CMB photons last scattered off free electrons (Thomson scattering), a process that does not depend on frequency.
During recombination, the newly formed neutral hydrogen and helium can also scatter photons: this is *Rayleigh scattering*.
Its cross-section grows steeply with frequency (∝ ν⁴), so high-frequency photons keep scattering a little longer.
The last scattering surface therefore becomes frequency dependent: at high frequency it sits slightly later and closer to us,
and the same physical scale is seen under a slightly larger angle.

The signal is small, but it carries information that the primary CMB alone cannot provide. In a series of three papers, we showed that:

- measuring Rayleigh scattering can significantly improve constraints on the number of relativistic species N<sub>eff</sub> and the sum of neutrino masses Σm<sub>ν</sub> ([Beringue et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2008.11688));
- Rayleigh anisotropies contain new primordial information, improving constraints on primordial non-Gaussianity by 30% or more ([Coulton et al. 2021](https://arxiv.org/abs/2010.10481));
- astrophysical foregrounds and the atmosphere make a detection very challenging for this decade's ground-based experiments, while a future CMB satellite should reach a high-significance measurement ([Zhu et al. 2022](https://arxiv.org/abs/2205.04496)).
